## PART ONE: THE WORLD BEFORE

[VISUAL: Archival - photographs of mammoth tusks, dire wolf skulls, giant ground sloth bones in museum displays]

**THE GOD (V.O.)**

The Ice Age was ending. But it was dying slowly.

For ten thousand years, humans had walked among giants. Woolly mammoths roamed the northern steppes. Giant short-faced bears, three times the size of grizzlies, hunted the forests of North America. In the caves and plains, humans thrived as hunters. They knew the herds. They knew the seasons. They knew how to survive in a world of ice.

[VISUAL: Veo 3.1 - cinematic reconstructed scene: small band of Ice Age hunters with spears, watching a mammoth herd move across tundra landscape at dawn, naturalistic and documentary-like]

[VISUAL: Graphic - world map with ice sheet extent, green areas showing habitable zones, population concentrations marked]

Then came a day that changed everything.

## PART TWO: THE IMPACT

[VISUAL: Archival - close-up of Younger Dryas Boundary layer in ice core or geological cross-section, showing the distinctive layer]

**THE GOD (V.O.)**

In the ice records of Greenland, of Antarctica, of the mountain glaciers of the world, there is a thin line.

A fingerprint of catastrophe.

The Younger Dryas Boundary. A layer of carbon, of ash, of microscopic spherules—tiny glass beads created in the heat of impact. Soot from fires. Markers of instant, planetary-scale trauma.

[VISUAL: Veo 3.1 - cinematic animation of meteor entering atmosphere, breaking apart, multiple impacts across northern landscape, fireballs, shockwaves]

[GRAPHIC: Animated diagram showing impact hypothesis - meteor/comet cluster entering atmosphere, airburst scenario over North America]

The evidence suggests it was not one strike. It was a swarm. A fragmented comet, breaking apart as it fell, raining down across the northern hemisphere.

[VISUAL: Archival - aerial photographs of impact craters (Barringer, Wolf Creek), geological formations that tell the story of ancient impacts]

The energy released in seconds exceeded every nuclear weapon humanity would ever build.

---

## PART THREE: EXTINCTION

[VISUAL: Graphic - timeline animation showing species extinction rates before and after 12,800 BCE, species disappearing as the line progresses]

**THE GOD (V.O.)**

The megafauna died. Not slowly. Not over generations. In a geological blink.

The woolly mammoths vanished. The giant ground sloths. The saber-toothed cats. The dire wolves. In North America alone, 35 genera of large animals went extinct. In Europe, Africa, Asia—the same pattern. The giants fell.

[VISUAL: Veo 3.1 - reconstructed scene: Ice Age landscape with thriving megafauna herds, then impact event, then desolate, burned landscape with dead animals, smoke on horizon]

[VISUAL: Archival - museum displays of extinction-era animal remains, artistic reconstructions of extinct species]

Some believe the impact itself killed them. Others argue the climate collapse that followed—the sudden return of ice age conditions in what should have been warming—starved them. Perhaps it was both. Perhaps it mattered little to the creatures dying.

What mattered was that the world changed.

---

## PART FOUR: THE YOUNGER DRYAS — CLIMATE CHAOS

[VISUAL: Graphic - animated climate data visualization showing temperature plunge, ice core data from Greenland showing the sudden cold anomaly]

**THE GOD (V.O.)**

The name is modest for what it describes. The Younger Dryas. A period of a thousand years in which the warming that should have been inevitable was... stopped. Reversed. The world, having begun to emerge from the Ice Age, plunged back into cold.

It was as if the planet itself flinched.

[VISUAL: Veo 3.1 - montage of environmental transformation: forests withering, glaciers advancing, herds moving or dying, human settlements becoming smaller and more desperate]

Temperatures in the north dropped ten degrees in a human lifetime. Not gradually. Suddenly. Within a few decades.

The climate models struggle with this. They search for mechanisms. An ocean circulation collapse triggered by freshwater from impact-melted glaciers flooding the Atlantic. A dust veil blocking the sun. A direct thermal pulse from the impact itself.

[GRAPHIC: Animated diagram of Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation disruption, freshwater input blocking thermohaline conveyor]

## PART FIVE: THE HUMAN RECKONING

[VISUAL: Archival - archaeological excavation footage, artifacts from pre-impact settlement sites, evidence of sudden abandonment]

**THE GOD (V.O.)**

The humans were fewer in number, more scattered. But they survived the impact itself.

What nearly broke them was what came after.

[VISUAL: Veo 3.1 - reconstructed scene: a group of hunters in landscape that is visibly changing—familiar hunting grounds becoming inhospitable, herds vanishing, a woman looking at a child with fear and hunger in her eyes]

Population collapsed. Settlements were abandoned. The cultural knowledge that had sustained them—where the herds move, when the seasons turn, which plants are safe—became useless. The herds were gone. The seasons had betrayed them. Everything they knew was wrong.

[VISUAL: Graphic - animated map showing human population density before and after impact, regions of decline highlighted]

For a thousand years, they endured. But the world had become alien.

---

## PART SIX: THE MYTHS REMEMBER

[VISUAL: Archival - ancient texts, cuneiform tablets, hieroglyphics, cave paintings from cultures around the world]

**THE GOD (V.O.)**

What is curious—what I have witnessed again and again—is that your species remembers.

Not with precision. Not with dates or evidence. But with the deep, wordless memory of trauma.

[VISUAL: Graphic - animated text montage of global flood myths and sky-fire myths, appearing on screen:]

The Sumerians spoke of the great flood that wiped the earth clean.

The Greeks told of Deucalion and the deluge that drowned the world.

The Hindus preserved the memory in the fire and water of the Pralaya.

The Maya carved the story into stone—the world cycle ending in catastrophic destruction.

The Indigenous peoples of the Americas held stories of the sky falling, of ice returning, of the world nearly ending.

[VISUAL: Veo 3.1 - cinematic reconstruction montage: various ancient cultures' peoples witnessing sky fire, floods, darkness, displacement, reimagined through their own cultural lens]

Were these memories of the impact? Of the Younger Dryas? Or older catastrophes? Or simply the universal language of trauma, told differently by each people?

I cannot answer. But I can observe: *they all remember the sky catching fire. They all remember the world almost ending.*

---

## PART SEVEN: THE AFTERMATH

[VISUAL: Archival - photographs of Göbekli Tepe, early Neolithic structures, evidence of human rebuilding and social reorganization]

**THE GOD (V.O.)**

When the cold finally released its grip—truly released it, around 9600 BCE—humans emerged changed.

They would never again wander purely as hunters. Within a few centuries, they began to settle. To plant. To build. At Göbekli Tepe in Anatolia, they raised stone towers and gathering places, as if saying: *We survived. We will not scatter again.*

[VISUAL: Veo 3.1 - reconstructed scene: early Neolithic settlement, people gathering at a ceremonial structure, sense of community and intentional rebuilding]

[VISUAL: Graphic - timeline showing transition from hunter-gatherer to agricultural societies, coinciding with Younger Dryas recovery period]

Was it the impact that drove them to civilization? Or did civilization's seeds fall in the ashes of apocalypse?

Perhaps both. Perhaps catastrophe teaches what comfort cannot.

## CLOSING

[VISUAL: Return to ice core archival footage, the thin line of the Younger Dryas Boundary visible]

**THE GOD (V.O.)**

The impact remains a mystery. Some will argue about the comet. Others demand proof. Still others deny it happened at all, insisting the extinction of the megafauna had other causes.

To me, these debates seem small.

[VISUAL: Veo 3.1 - wide shot of modern landscape, human civilization visible in the distance, sense of time passing]

What matters is this: *In a moment, the world changed. Civilizations that could not adapt perished. Those with flexibility, resourcefulness, social bonds—they survived.*
