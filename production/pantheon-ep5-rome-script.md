# Pantheon — Episode 5: The Last Roman
## How the Empire That Never Lost Finally Did

*Narration script, verbatim, in beat order. Beat IDs are inline cross-reference markers — read the prose through, ignore the brackets. Narrator throughout is THE GOD (V.O.).*

---

## COLD OPEN

**THE GOD (V.O.)**

[B001] On a late summer day in the year four hundred and seventy-six, a soldier walked into a palace in the Italian city of Ravenna and told a sixteen-year-old boy that he was no longer emperor of Rome. [B002] The boy's name was Romulus. His enemies, not without irony, called him Augustulus — Little Augustus. [B003] He did not die. He was not even imprisoned. He was given an estate on the Bay of Naples and a yearly pension, and the empire that had ruled the Mediterranean world for five hundred years simply stopped having a man in Italy who could call himself its emperor. [B004] No battle marked the day. No great fire. Most people in the city of Rome did not learn of it for weeks. [B005] I was there for the beginning of that empire. I watched it swallow Gaul, and Egypt, and the kingdom of Kush's own neighbors on the Nile, and I watched a queen with one eye force a Roman emperor to the bargaining table and walk away with her freedom. [B006] I am telling you now how the empire that did that to everyone else finally had it done to itself. [B007] Not in one day. In a hundred years of them.

---

## 2. The Peace That Could Not Last

**THE GOD (V.O.)**

[B008] For two centuries, Rome's power was so complete that its own citizens forgot it could end. [B009] One road system connected Britain to the Euphrates. One currency moved through every port. A traveler could cross from Spain to Syria and never leave Roman law. [B010] The historians of the day called it the Roman Peace, and for a long while, it deserved the name. [B011] But an empire that size has borders longer than any army can hold forever, and beyond those borders, the world was not standing still. [B012] Through the third century after the common era, the cracks showed first. Plague emptied cities. Soldiers made and unmade emperors faster than coins could be struck with their faces. For a few decades, there were years when Rome had a new ruler almost every season. [B013] The empire did not fall then. It reorganized. It split its rule in two — an eastern half, rich and walled behind the new capital at Constantinople, and a western half, older, poorer, and far more exposed. [B014] Both halves called themselves Rome. Only one of them would see the next century out.

---

## 3. A People Running from Something Worse

**THE GOD (V.O.)**

[B015] In the year three hundred seventy-six, Roman officers on the Danube frontier watched a sight they had never seen: tens of thousands of Gothic people, whole families, wagons, elders, children, begging to be let inside the empire. [B016] They were not raiders. They were refugees. [B017] Something had driven them out of their homeland on the steppes north of the Black Sea, and that something had a name Rome would come to know well: the Huns, horse-warriors out of Central Asia whose archery and mobility nothing in the Gothic world could withstand. [B018] Rome said yes. Let them cross. It was, on its own terms, not an unreasonable decision — Gothic warriors could fill the legions, Gothic farmers could work empty land. [B019] But the empire did it badly. Roman officials meant to feed and settle them instead cheated them, sold them dog meat at famine prices, took their children as slaves for payment. [B020] Desperate people, betrayed by the power that was supposed to protect them, do not stay peaceful for long. [B021] By three hundred seventy-eight, the Goths were not refugees anymore. They were an army inside Rome's own borders. [B022] The Roman emperor Valens marched out from Constantinople to put them down before his nephew's reinforcements could arrive and share the glory. [B023] Near the city of Adrianople, he found the Goths drawn up and waiting. [B024] The battle lasted an afternoon. Roman cavalry rode in late and scattered; the infantry was boxed in, crushed from every side, and by nightfall perhaps two-thirds of the eastern Roman field army lay dead on the plain, the emperor Valens among them — his body never recovered, lost in the chaos or burned alive in a farmhouse where the wounded had taken shelter. [B025] No Roman emperor had died in battle against a foreign enemy in centuries. [B026] A Roman historian who wrote of that day called it the worst defeat since Hannibal. He understated it.

---

## 4. The Sack

**THE GOD (V.O.)**

[B027] The Goths who broke Rome at Adrianople did not go away. For a generation they moved through the Balkans and Greece, sometimes at war with the empire, sometimes serving in its armies, always searching for a settlement Rome never fully honored. [B028] Their leader for much of this was a man named Alaric — born on an island in the Danube delta, raised in a kingdom of Romans and Goths tangled together, a man who wanted not to destroy Rome but to be given an honored place inside it: land for his people, a Roman command worthy of his rank. [B029] Three times he marched on Italy to make his case. Three times Rome's generals bled him, bribed him, or outlasted him. [B030] The fourth time, in the year four hundred ten, nothing stopped him. [B031] On the night of August the twenty-fourth, enslaved people inside Rome itself — by one account, the very people the city depended on to run its households — opened the Salarian Gate from within. [B032] For the first time in almost eight hundred years, an army that was not Rome's own stood inside the walls of Rome. [B033] What followed was three days of looting, not the annihilation later legend made of it — Alaric's own men were Christian, as was most of the city, and churches were spared by his order — but the shock was not in the violence. It was in the fact of it happening at all. [B034] A scholar far away in the Holy Land, hearing the news, wrote that his voice failed him and sobs cut short his words: the city that had taken the whole world captive was itself taken. [B035] Alaric did not live to see what came of it. He died of fever months later, marching south, and his men are said to have diverted a river to bury him in its bed with his treasures, then killed the enslaved laborers who had dug the grave, so that no living person could say where a conqueror of Rome lay at rest.

---

## 5. The Scourge and the General

**THE GOD (V.O.)**

[B036] The same pressure that had pushed the Goths across the Danube a lifetime earlier eventually arrived in person. [B037] By the fourteen-forties, a Hunnic king named Attila ruled an empire of his own stretching from the Rhine to the steppes of Central Asia, and both Roman empires paid him tribute in gold simply to keep him from marching. [B038] When he marched anyway, in four hundred fifty-one, he came west into Gaul — and there, Rome found one general still capable of meeting him. [B039] Flavius Aetius had spent his youth as a hostage among the Huns himself; he knew their tactics, and in some years he had even been their ally against Rome's other enemies. [B040] Now he built the only kind of army the dying western empire could still raise: Romans, yes, but beside them Visigoths, Franks, and other peoples the empire had once called barbarians, fighting under Roman command because Attila threatened them all alike. [B041] On the Catalaunian Plains, that patchwork army met Attila's horde in what ancient writers called one of the bloodiest single-day battles the world had seen. [B042] Attila was checked, not destroyed. He withdrew — and the following year invaded Italy instead, burning cities, until, by tradition, a meeting with the bishop of Rome persuaded him to turn back, his army already weakened by hunger and disease. [B043] Attila died not long after, on his own wedding night, by a burst blood vessel, not a Roman sword. [B044] Aetius, the general who had held the line against him, did not outlive him by much. The reigning emperor, fearing a man more capable than himself, stabbed him to death with his own hand in four hundred fifty-four. [B045] A courtier is said to have told the emperor he had just cut off his right hand with his left. Within a year, the emperor too was dead, killed by Aetius's former bodyguards.

---

## 6. Fourteen Days

**THE GOD (V.O.)**

[B046] The empire that killed its last great general had little left to defend itself with. [B047] In four hundred fifty-five, a Vandal king named Gaiseric sailed from North Africa — a kingdom the Vandals had carved for themselves a generation earlier — and landed at the mouth of the Tiber. [B048] Rome's new bishop, Leo, rode out to meet him as he once had met Attila, and won one concession: no massacre, no burning. [B049] What Gaiseric's men took instead, over fourteen methodical days, was everything that could be carried — gold, silver, half the gilded bronze roof tiles of the Temple of Jupiter, imperial treasures, the emperor's own widow and daughters, taken as captives to Carthage. [B050] It is from this sack, not Alaric's, that a later age drew the word for wanton destruction of beautiful things — vandalism — a label that outlived the kingdom it was coined to insult by well over a thousand years. [B051] After Gaiseric sailed home, the western throne became a title more than a power. [B052] Nine men wore the crown in the next twenty-one years. Several were killed. Several were puppets of whichever barbarian general commanded the loyalty of what remained of the army. [B053] The empire of the west had not been conquered in one great battle. It had been cashed out, piece by piece, by men who found it was worth more as plunder and pension than as a government worth saving.

---

## 7. The Boy Who Did Not Fight Back

**THE GOD (V.O.)**

[B054] By four hundred seventy-five, the army defending Italy was itself made up mostly of soldiers from Germanic peoples — men who had fought for Rome for generations and wanted, in the end, the same thing Alaric had asked for seventy years before: land of their own, recognized and legal, inside the empire they served. [B055] Rome's last functioning general refused them. [B056] His own officer corps turned on him. A commander named Odoacer, himself of mixed barbarian descent, was raised on a shield by his soldiers and given the general's job, and within weeks, the general was dead and the boy-emperor his faction had installed — Romulus Augustulus — had nothing left to rule in whose name. [B057] Odoacer did not crown himself emperor of the west. He did something stranger. He sent the imperial regalia east, to Constantinople, with a message: the west no longer needed an emperor of its own. One Roman emperor, ruling from the east, was enough. [B058] The eastern emperor accepted the symbols and, with no army to spare for Italy, accepted the arrangement too. [B059] So ended, by the only date historians could ever agree to put a pin in, the Western Roman Empire. [B060] Romulus Augustulus lived on for decades in his villa on the coast, the last of his name, a pensioner of the kingdom that had replaced him, dying of old age in a world where Rome still stood, and no one who called himself its emperor lived in it anymore.

---

## 8. What Actually Fell

**THE GOD (V.O.)**

[B061] You have been taught this was an ending. I want to be more careful with you than that. [B062] The eastern half of the empire did not fall in four hundred seventy-six. It called itself Rome for another thousand years, until a different army took Constantinople in fourteen fifty-three — and some of its own people, to the end, never stopped calling themselves Romans. [B063] In Italy and Gaul and Spain, the kingdoms that replaced the western empire kept Roman law on their books, Roman bishops in their cities, Roman roads under their feet, Latin on their tongues reshaping itself, generation by generation, into French and Spanish and Italian. [B064] Odoacer himself ruled Italy for the Roman Senate's benefit, in Roman administrative forms, for seventeen more years, until a different Gothic king killed him at a banquet and took his place doing much the same thing. [B065] The empire did not vanish. Its single throne broke into many, and the idea of it proved harder to kill than the government that had once enforced it. [B066] For a thousand years afterward, kings from Germany to Russia would take the word Caesar for their own titles — Kaiser, Tsar — reaching back for a legitimacy they thought had died in that palace at Ravenna. [B067] It had not died. It had simply stopped belonging to any one throne. [B068] I have watched kingdoms erase their predecessors with a chisel, cutting a name from stone so thoroughly that only the holes remembered the king had been there. [B069] Rome's fall was the opposite kind of vanishing. Everyone kept the name. Almost no one kept the thing the name once meant. [B070] Decide for yourself, then, which is the more thorough way to disappear.
