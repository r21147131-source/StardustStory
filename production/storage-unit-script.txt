Someone paid twenty dollars for a storage locker. A missing woman was inside.
People treat storage auctions like a scratch-off ticket. You don’t get to rummage first. You get a photo, a unit size, and a one-line description. You put money down. You own whatever is sitting in the dark. As-is. No returns. No “I didn’t know.”
That is the whole pitch. That is why the shows exist. That is why a guy will drive two hours for a locker he has never stood inside.
In The Dalles, Oregon, a man from Yakima paid about twenty dollars for a five-by-five at the U-Haul on West 6th Street. The listing was almost bragging. A big tote. Very heavy. Sealed with zip ties.
He said later it felt like a casino. You never know what’s in there.
On July 28th, 2025, he found out.
He rolled the door. The smell hit him in a way leftover clothes do not hit you. He did the one smart thing in this entire story. He called the police.
They got a warrant. They opened the tote on the concrete.
Inside, according to court records, was a woman who had been missing for more than a year.
Her name was Meihong He. People who loved her called her Kiki.
Kiki was thirty-nine. She was originally from China. She moved through California, Montana, and the towns along the Columbia. She traveled for work. She kept a small circle. She was not a headline waiting to happen. She was a person with a wedding date on the calendar.
In the spring of 2024 she was engaged.
Court records say she and Richard Michael Robinson — Mike, fifty-nine, out of The Dalles and Klickitat County, Washington — had been together about a year. The wedding was set for May 30th, 2024.
Four days before that date, she disappeared.
I want you to sit with that for a second. Not the tote yet. The date. Invitations energy. A dress, or at least a plan. And then a story about a fishing trip that had to hold for fourteen months.
Robinson told people he dropped Kiki off on May 25th, 2024, so she could go fishing with friends near Highway 14.
That became the official last sighting. A drop-off. Friends. A river. A woman who did not come back.
She was reported missing in early June. Family asked the questions families ask. He kept saying he was looking.
For a year and change, that version of events just sat there. No body. No crime scene the public could point to. Just a gap where a person used to be, and a fiancé with a sentence that sounded ordinary enough to repeat.
If you have ever watched a missing-person case from the outside, you already know how this works. The first story is the one that freezes. Everyone else has to climb over it.
Then a locker went into default.
This is the part that makes people send the video to a group chat.
A storage company is not a police department. It is a landlord with roll-up doors. If you stop paying, they follow a script written for couches and Christmas decorations. Notice. Lien. Auction. The buyer purchases contents, not a biography of the last person who had the key.
Prosecutors say Robinson rented that outdoor U-Haul unit in The Dalles — the rows by the KFC — shortly after Kiki vanished. Court records say he made one payment. Then nothing.
The unit sat there. Unpaid. Unremarkable. Unit 0117. Five by five. Not climate controlled.
When it hit the auction site, the description read like a warning written by someone who did not know they were writing a warning. A big tote. Very heavy. Sealed with zip ties. Two bids. Winning number: twenty dollars.
The buyer, identified in later local reporting as Harmon, did not even clear it the day the gavel fell. Some accounts say the unit was auctioned in the spring and claimed in July. The listing that still sits online shows an end date of July 28th, 2025, and a last bid of twenty. Either way, the important fact is the same. There was a lag. The tote waited.
That lag is why a twenty-dollar locker became a crime scene instead of a dump run.
Just before 9:40 in the morning on July 29th, Harmon called The Dalles police and reported a suspicious circumstance. Officers looked at the tote, decided this was not a hoarder’s leftover protein powder, and called in the Mid-Columbia Major Crimes Team. Oregon State Police crime lab came to the locker. A Wasco County judge signed a warrant.
They opened the box on purpose, with gloves, on camera, the way you are supposed to.
What they found is in the file. We are going to stay with the file.
Court records describe a seventy-seven-gallon plastic tote. Black body. Mismatched yellow lid. Wrapped in layers of clear plastic. About a dozen zip ties. A ratchet strap so the lid would not lift. Charcoal briquettes packed around the contents, which prosecutors say was meant to hide the smell of a body that had been there through a full year of Oregon weather.
The Oregon medical examiner ruled the death a homicide by asphyxiation. A preliminary finding, repeated in court and in local investigations, said she was likely still alive when she was placed in the tote.
That sentence is going to live in the comments. Hold it correctly. It is a medical finding and a prosecution claim in a case that has not gone to a jury. It is not a verdict.
Two days later, fingerprints did what fourteen months of a fishing story could not.
It was Kiki.
Next of kin were told. The missing-person flyer could come down. The locker could not go back to being a joke about treasure hunting.
This is where the auction story stops being a curiosity and becomes the reason you will think about your own locker on the way to work.
Prosecutors say Robinson rented an excavator in the same window he rented the unit. Two days after the machine, they say, he crossed the Columbia into Oregon and paid for 0117. One payment. Then silence. If you are looking for a moment that sounds like a man changing plans, that is the one the district attorney put on the record.
After he was arrested, local investigators reported a jailhouse interview. According to that account, Robinson admitted he had lied from the beginning. He told detectives, they say, that Kiki died inside his trailer, and that he then drove around for days with her body in the bed of his pickup — including a trip to Tacoma for his daughter’s birthday — before the locker.
He has pleaded not guilty. A statement in a jail is not a conviction. Treat it as what it is: something prosecutors will try to use, and something a defense will try to fence off.
What is not in dispute on the public timeline is colder.
While that tote sat behind a roll-up door next to a fast-food sign, the story in the living world was still a fishing trip. Family still asking. A wedding date that had already passed. A registered sex offender with a domestic-violence history still moving between states.
On August 25th, 2025, U.S. Marshals arrested him in Colorado, in the Berthoud and Fort Collins area, on a warrant for failing to register as a sex offender. Detectives had already connected him to Kiki. The registration warrant was the door they could legally kick. They interviewed him. Then the charges landed in two states.
Washington: second-degree murder and first-degree kidnapping. That is the alleged killing, on the Washington side of the river.
Oregon: second-degree abuse of a corpse. That is the tote, the charcoal, the year in unit 0117.
Two states, because the alleged crime and the alleged hiding did not clock in at the same address.
As of the latest public hearings, he has continued to plead not guilty. The case is not over. If you came here for a gavel, you are early. If you came here for the part that is already true, you have it.
A missing woman was stored like abandoned property. The first person who treated the tote like evidence was the man who bought it for the price of a pizza.
I am not going to turn this into a countdown of every body ever found in a locker. That content exists. It is usually sloppy. It usually forgets the names.
What matters is the mechanic, because the mechanic is still legal and still running in your zip code.
Rent lapses. A company follows the statute. A photo goes up. A bidder pays cash. Nobody is required to X-ray the Rubbermaid. The first investigator is whoever can stand the smell long enough to make a phone call.
Different decades. Different states. Same sentence in the police blotter: the buyer opened the unit and called 911.
You do not need a legend about cursed storage. You need the lien law and a person willing to stop paying.
That is the part that travels. Not the tote. The fact that the tote could be sold.
Kiki was supposed to get married at the end of May.
By early June, according to prosecutors, she was in a plastic tote in a five-by-five on West 6th Street.
For a year the locker cost almost nothing to ignore. Then it cost twenty dollars to own.
The buyer thought he was gambling on someone else’s leftover life. He was. Just not the way the television version sells it.
Robinson has not been convicted. The file is still moving. What is already on the table does not need extra mythology.
A woman went missing on a story about a fishing trip.

A unit went to auction on a story about unpaid rent.

The only reason her name came back was that somebody won the bid.
If you rent a locker, or you have ever watched one of those auctions and thought what’s the worst that could be in there — you already know.
It is not furniture.
It is the thing nobody priced, because the system is built to sell the box, not to ask what the box is for.
