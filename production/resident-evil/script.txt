RESIDENT EVIL (2026) — Hollywood finally figured out video game movies
Recording script as voiced; sections separated by '—— ——'.


For more than twenty years...

Hollywood has been trying to answer one question:

How do you turn a video game into a great movie?

And for more than twenty years...

the answer has usually been:

"Apparently, not like that."

But something very strange just happened.

A new Resident Evil movie came out.

And instead of simply adapting one of the games...

director Zach Cregger did something much more interesting.

He tried to make the audience feel like they were playing one.

And that difference might explain why this movie is connecting with both horror audiences and gamers.

—— ——

Let's rewind.

Resident Evil has been around since 1996.

The original game helped establish an entire genre of survival horror.

But the concept was actually incredibly simple.

You are trapped somewhere terrifying.

You don't have enough resources.

You don't know what's around the next corner.

You have to explore.

You have to solve problems.

And sometimes...

you have to run.

Not fight.

Run.

Because you're not a superhero.

You're just trying to survive.

That distinction is everything.

—— ——

Over the years, the Resident Evil games became enormous.

Zombies.

Mutants.

Viruses.

Umbrella.

Secret laboratories.

Biological weapons.

Massive conspiracies.

And some of the most recognizable characters in gaming.

Leon Kennedy.

Claire Redfield.

Jill Valentine.

Chris Redfield.

And many more.

So when Hollywood adapted Resident Evil for theaters, there was an obvious temptation.

Take the recognizable characters.

Take the monsters.

Take the action.

Make it bigger.

Make it louder.

And put it on a giant screen.

But there's a problem.

That can give you a movie that looks like Resident Evil...

without actually feeling like Resident Evil.

—— ——

That's where Zach Cregger comes in.

Cregger became known for horror movies like Barbarian and Weapons.

And instead of simply asking:

"Which game should I adapt?"

he seems to have asked a different question:

"What does it actually feel like to play Resident Evil?"

That changes everything.

Because playing a survival-horror game isn't just about the story.

It's about your relationship with the environment.

You enter a room.

You don't know what's inside.

You hear something.

You don't know where it came from.

You have a limited amount of ammunition.

You have limited supplies.

You don't want to waste them.

You start thinking:

"Can I take this enemy?"

"Should I run?"

"What's behind that door?"

"Is this safe?"

And then...

you hear something.

And suddenly you don't want to open the door anymore.

That's the experience.

—— ——

So instead of making another superhuman action hero...

Cregger created Bryan.

Played by Austin Abrams.

Bryan is a medical courier.

He's not Leon Kennedy.

He's not Chris Redfield.

He's not a trained special operative.

He's an ordinary person who gets thrown into an extraordinary nightmare.

And that makes the situation much scarier.

Because if Bryan gets hurt...

there's no reason to assume he's going to magically recover.

If he runs out of ammunition...

he doesn't have an unlimited arsenal hidden somewhere.

If something stronger than him appears...

he has a very obvious option.

Run.

—— ——

And this is where the movie's approach becomes fascinating.

Because some fans initially questioned the decision.

Where are Leon and Claire?

Why isn't this directly adapting one of the games?

Why create a new character?

Why change the story?

Those are legitimate questions.

But there's another way to look at it.

What if the goal isn't to adapt a specific Resident Evil game?

What if the goal is to adapt the experience of playing one?

That's a completely different challenge.

And suddenly the choices make sense.

—— ——

Think about video games.

A game can take hours.

A movie usually has around two hours.

A game can let you wander around.

A movie has to control pacing.

A game can make you solve a puzzle yourself.

A movie has to show you the story.

A game can give you an inventory.

A movie doesn't have an inventory screen.

So filmmakers have to find another way to translate those mechanics.

And Cregger's solution is basically:

Turn game mechanics into cinematic tension.

—— ——

Limited ammunition becomes suspense.

Exploration becomes discovery.

Inventory management becomes resource anxiety.

Mission objectives become narrative structure.

Enemies become obstacles that have to be strategically handled.

And the environment becomes part of the story.

The movie reportedly uses a structure that resembles the progression of a game, moving through different "levels" or stages as Bryan tries to survive.

That's an incredibly smart way to approach a game adaptation.

Because it doesn't require the audience to know every piece of lore.

You don't need to know exactly what happened in Resident Evil 2.

You don't need to remember every character from Resident Evil 4.

You just need to understand one thing:

Something terrible happened.

And Bryan needs to survive it.

—— ——

That's probably why the movie can work for people who have never played the games.

And that's important.

Because the biggest challenge with video game adaptations is accessibility.

If you make the movie too faithful...

new viewers can feel lost.

If you make it too different...

fans can feel betrayed.

Cregger appears to have found a third option.

Keep the DNA.

Change the story.

—— ——

And the DNA is everywhere.

Raccoon City.

The T-virus.

Umbrella.

Zombies.

Monsters.

Scavenging.

Limited resources.

Typewriters.

Environmental storytelling.

Mission-like progression.

The feeling that opening a door could be the worst decision you've made all night.

These aren't random references.

They're pieces of the experience.

—— ——

And that's why the movie's reception has been so interesting.

Critics and audiences have responded strongly.

Rotten Tomatoes currently lists the movie at 96% from critics and 91% from verified audience ratings.

And commercially...

the movie opened to around $60 million domestically, with roughly $108.3 million worldwide in its opening weekend.

That's particularly notable because Resident Evil isn't a new property.

This isn't the first attempt.

It's another reboot of a franchise that has already produced multiple live-action and animated films.

And yet audiences showed up.

—— ——

But here's the really interesting part.

The movie's success could point toward a larger change in Hollywood.

For years, video game adaptations were treated almost like cursed projects.

Studios would buy the rights.

Hire writers.

Change the characters.

Change the tone.

Change the story.

And then wonder why fans didn't recognize the thing they loved.

But the industry has slowly learned something.

A video game isn't just its plot.

A game has mechanics.

A game has rhythm.

A game has player agency.

A game has atmosphere.

A game has a specific emotional experience.

And those things can sometimes matter more than copying the storyline.

—— ——

Think about it.

If someone loves Resident Evil 4, what do they actually remember?

Sure, they remember characters.

They remember events.

But they also remember walking through dark environments.

They remember conserving ammunition.

They remember hearing an enemy before seeing it.

They remember suddenly realizing they're surrounded.

They remember the feeling of being underpowered.

They remember opening a door and wondering what was waiting on the other side.

That's what Cregger is trying to recreate.

Not necessarily the exact game...

but the feeling of playing one.

—— ——

And that's why the absence of Leon or Claire might actually be part of the experiment.

Because if you simply put Leon into another Resident Evil movie...

the audience already knows he's capable.

He's a trained survivor.

He's cool.

He's experienced.

He knows what he's doing.

Bryan doesn't.

And that creates uncertainty.

You don't know exactly what he's capable of.

He doesn't know what he's doing.

And therefore...

neither does the audience.

That's a powerful horror tool.

—— ——

There's also something refreshing about making the protagonist an ordinary person.

Horror works best when the audience can imagine themselves in the situation.

If Superman walks into a zombie apocalypse...

it's not particularly scary.

If an ordinary medical courier walks into one...

that's different.

Because suddenly you start thinking:

What would I do?

Would I hide?

Would I run?

Would I try to find a weapon?

Would I waste ammunition?

Would I open that door?

Would I trust that person?

Those are player questions.

And that's exactly the point.

—— ——

The movie also embraces something else the games have always understood:

horror and humor can exist together.

Resident Evil has never been purely serious.

The franchise can be ridiculous.

It can be grotesque.

It can be funny.

It can be terrifying.

Sometimes all within the same five minutes.

And Cregger's background in horror comedy helps explain why that tone works.

The movie isn't trying to be miserable for two hours.

It's trying to entertain you.

Sometimes it scares you.

Sometimes it makes you laugh.

Sometimes it throws something completely insane at you.

And then...

it keeps moving.

—— ——

That's another reason the runtime matters.

The movie is only around 90 minutes.

That's short by modern blockbuster standards.

But for this particular concept...

that can actually work.

It gives the film the momentum of a game session.

You're not spending an hour watching characters explain the mythology.

You're moving.

You survive one thing.

Then another.

Then another.

The next objective appears.

The situation gets worse.

And suddenly you're at the end.

That pace is very different from the sprawling cinematic universes Hollywood has become accustomed to.

—— ——

And there's a bigger lesson here.

Maybe the best video game adaptations shouldn't try to become movies in the traditional sense.

Maybe they should figure out what makes games special...

and then translate that into cinema.

Resident Evil doesn't have to give you a controller.

It just needs to make your brain behave as though you have one.

When Bryan enters a room...

you should start scanning the environment.

When ammunition becomes scarce...

you should start calculating.

When a monster appears...

you should start looking for an escape route.

When the movie presents a new location...

you should wonder:

What's going to happen here?

That's game logic.

But it works beautifully as movie logic.

—— ——

And this might explain why the new Resident Evil arrived at such an interesting moment.

Video game adaptations are changing.

Audiences have become more demanding.

Gamers aren't automatically impressed just because a movie uses the name of a famous game.

They want to see that filmmakers understand why the game mattered.

And that doesn't necessarily mean copying everything.

It can mean understanding the spirit of it.

That's what Cregger appears to have prioritized.

The film takes a new story...

but places that story inside the emotional machinery of Resident Evil.

—— ——

And now we're left with a much bigger question.

Could this be the new formula for video game movies?

Not:

"How do we recreate the game?"

But:

"How do we recreate the experience?"

Because those are completely different questions.

And if the answer is the second one...

Hollywood suddenly has a lot more possibilities.

You don't have to recreate every mission.

You don't have to force every famous character into the movie.

You don't have to turn the film into a two-hour Easter egg hunt.

You can take the world...

take the mechanics...

take the atmosphere...

and tell a new story.

—— ——

That's what makes Resident Evil 2026 interesting.

It's not simply another reboot.

It's an experiment in adaptation.

And the experiment seems to be working.

The movie has already become one of the franchise's biggest domestic performers, while its reception has been notably stronger than many previous entries.

But perhaps the most important achievement is simpler.

It makes the audience feel vulnerable.

And vulnerability is the foundation of survival horror.

—— ——

Because at the end of the day...

Resident Evil was never really about zombies.

It wasn't really about viruses.

It wasn't even really about Umbrella.

Those things are the decoration.

The core experience is this:

You're somewhere you don't understand.

Something is hunting you.

You don't have enough resources.

You don't know what's coming next.

And you have to keep moving.

That's survival horror.

And that's what Cregger seems to understand.

—— ——

For twenty years, Hollywood kept asking:

"How do we make a Resident Evil movie?"

Maybe they were asking the wrong question.

The better question was:

"How do we make people feel like they're trapped inside Resident Evil?"

And in 2026...

Zach Cregger may have finally found an answer.

Not by making another movie exactly like the games.

But by making a movie that understands...

why people love playing them.

The next era of video game movies might not be about faithful adaptations.

It might be about faithful experiences.

And if that's true...

Resident Evil may have just opened the door.
