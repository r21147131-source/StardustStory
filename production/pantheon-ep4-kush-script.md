# Pantheon — Episode 4: The Forgotten Ones
## The Pharaohs Egypt Tried to Erase

## COLD OPEN

**THE GOD (V.O.)**

[B001] There is a monument beside the Nile, at the foot of a sacred mountain. [B002] A tall slab of dark stone, covered in hieroglyphs from top to bottom. It tells how a king conquered Egypt. [B003] And on that stone, someone went back and cut the king out. [B004] His name was chiseled away. His figure was scraped from the carving. [B005] The story was left standing, as though the stone itself refused to forget what the living had decided to. [B006] I know who he was. I watched him come north. [B007] His name was Piye. He did not come from Egypt. [B008] He came from Kush, the kingdom to the south, which Egypt had spent centuries calling wretched and conquered. [B009] Within one generation, his family held the Two Lands together and ruled the largest Nile empire since the age of Ramesses. [B010] Egypt answered, in time. Not only with armies. With chisels. [B011] This is the story of the pharaohs Egypt tried to erase. [B012] But to understand the king, you first have to understand the mountain, and the very long humiliation that came before it.

## 2. The Land Between

**THE GOD (V.O.)**

[B013] For hundreds of years, Egypt owned Kush. Egyptian governors ran the gold mines of Nubia. [B014] Egyptian temples rose on Kushite soil. [B015] Egyptian kings carved their boasts about trampling the south, and Kush paid in gold, in ivory, in ebony, and in soldiers. [B016] I watched that arrangement last longer than most empires do. [B017] Then, around the year one thousand and seventy before the common era, the old order cracked. Egypt split apart. [B018] Priests ruled in Thebes. Chiefs of Libyan descent carved the north into small kingdoms, and several of them took the title of king. There were many kings, and none of them was strong. [B019] In the south, Kush did something Egypt had not planned for. It did not shatter. It grew. [B020] Its rulers made their home at Napata, beneath a flat-topped mountain called Jebel Barkal. [B021] Egyptians had always said the god Amun lived inside that mountain. The Kushites believed it too. They did not see themselves as outsiders storming a sacred land. They saw themselves as the people who had kept the old faith while Egypt let it fray. [B022] A prince named Alara is remembered as the founder of this line, the first ruler of Kush whose name has come down to us. His successor Kashta reached into Upper Egypt. [B023] And Kashta's son Piye inherited a kingdom with a great army, a deep faith, and the finest horses of the age. [B024] Meanwhile the north was tearing itself apart. And in the Delta city of Sais, one chief had decided to end that, by conquering everything himself. [B025] He chose the wrong year, and he chose the wrong neighbor.

## 3. The Storm from the South

**THE GOD (V.O.)**

[B026] His name was Tefnakht. He gathered the lords of the Delta into a coalition, and he marched south. [B027] Memphis, the ancient capital, fell to him. His army laid siege to Herakleopolis, a city loyal to Piye. [B028] If Tefnakht had won, there would have been no Kushite pharaohs. There would have been a Delta king, and a footnote. [B029] Piye was at Napata, in the twentieth year of his reign, when the news reached him. [B030] He sent his army north first, and then he followed himself. [B031] His orders to his commanders survive on the stone, and they read like the orders of a man who cared how a war was won. [B032] His forces won on the river and won again on land. [B033] Then a king named Nimlot of Hermopolis, who had been Piye's ally, abandoned him and joined Tefnakht. [B034] Piye besieged Hermopolis until it surrendered. Nimlot came out with gifts: gold, a sistrum, and a horse. [B035] Then Piye walked into the royal stables. His horses had been starved. Of everything Nimlot had done, it was this that Piye could not forgive. He said so, and the stone records it. [B036] That tells you who Piye was. Devout toward his gods. Tender toward his horses. Hard when he had to be. His own inscription calls him all of these things. And he was a very good general. [B037] Then came Memphis. Tefnakht slipped back inside the city and brought the garrison to eight thousand men. The walls were high. The river guarded one side. [B038] Piye's commanders urged a long siege. [B039] Piye struck the harbor instead. [B040] His ships and soldiers seized every vessel moored along the quay, and Memphis was stormed from the water. [B041] When the city fell, Piye did something the Delta lords did not expect. He went to the temple of Ptah, the god of the city, and gave thanks. He presented himself not as a raider but as a restorer, the pharaoh Egypt had been missing. [B042] One by one, the princes of the Delta came to him with tribute. [B043] They are carved on the stele, kneeling. [B044] Only Tefnakht stayed away. He sent a messenger with an oath of loyalty, and he never stood before Piye himself. [B045] Then Piye did the strangest thing of all. He loaded his ships with silver, gold, and copper, and sailed home. [B046] He made his sister Amenirdis the God's Wife of Amun at Thebes, which tied the great temples of the south to his family. [B047] But he went back to Kush, and so far as we know, he never ruled Egypt from within it. [B048] When he died, he was buried beneath a pyramid, a tomb style Egypt itself had abandoned centuries before, and eight of his horses were buried beside him. [B049 — cutaway insert, continues the same line, no new narration] [B050] His successors built on what he had won. And one of them would build as no king had in generations.

## 4. The Golden Age

**THE GOD (V.O.)**

[B051] Piye's successors ruled from Memphis, and the dynasty settled in. [B052] Then came Taharqa. [B053] Taharqa was a young prince when he first came north. His mother, Queen Abar, stayed behind in Kush for years while he rose in Egypt. [B054] When he became pharaoh and they were finally reunited, a stone in her homeland recorded the rejoicing. [B055] I mention this because it reminds you that these were people, not statues. [B056] Under Taharqa, the Nile valley was one kingdom from the Delta to deep in Kush. [B057] He restored temples at Karnak, and built new ones at Kawa and at Jebel Barkal. [B058] He revived the old sculptural styles and the old religious forms with great care. The people Egypt had called foreigners were rebuilding Egypt's own past, stone by stone. [B059] He built a pyramid at Nuri, the largest in all of Nubia. [B060] And his name survives even in the Hebrew Bible, as a king of Kush who marched out to fight the Assyrians. [B061] He had reason to march. For the first time, Egypt faced a rival it could not absorb or outlast. [B062] Assyria, to the east, had iron weapons, siege engines, and a policy of terror, and its kings counted their glory in cities burned. [B063] I had watched Egypt survive the Hyksos. I had watched it survive the Libyans, and the Sea Peoples. [B064] But Assyria was not like the others. It did not want a share. It wanted everything. [B065] Taharqa held the line. For a while.

## 5. The Spear of Assyria

**THE GOD (V.O.)**

[B066] In six hundred seventy-one before the common era, the Assyrian king Esarhaddon marched on Memphis, and Memphis fell. [B067] Members of the royal family were taken captive. [B068] Taharqa fled south. [B069] He returned. Egypt rebelled against its new masters. Esarhaddon set out to deal with it again and died on the road. [B070] His son Ashurbanipal finished the work, and drove Taharqa out of Egypt for good. [B071] Taharqa died at Napata, in six hundred sixty-four. [B072] His nephew Tantamani took the crown. [B073] Tantamani wrote of a dream, in which two serpents appeared, one at each side of him. He understood it to mean that both Upper and Lower Egypt were his. [B074] He marched north. He took Memphis. For a brief moment, the double kingdom lived again. [B075] Ashurbanipal came back. [B076] Thebes fell, and was sacked, in six hundred sixty-three, its temples stripped of their gold and its treasures carried to Nineveh. The ancient world remembered that day for centuries. [B077] An Assyrian inscription boasts that the king lifted his spear against Egypt and Kush and showed his power. [B078] Tantamani withdrew to Kush. [B079] In Egypt, an Assyrian ally from Sais named Psamtik founded a new dynasty. The twenty-fifth dynasty was over. [B080] The Egyptians who ruled next did not forget who had held their throne. [B081] Later kings of that line, Psamtik's descendants, sent masons through the monuments with chisels. Names were cut from walls at Karnak. [B082] Figures were scraped from temple carvings. [B083] On stones across the country, the Kushite kings were removed from the record. [B084] And then, a little over seventy years after Tantamani retreated, an Egyptian army marched south and struck the Kushite heartland at Napata. [B085] Slowly, the Kushite court moved its center further up the river, to a city called Meroe. [B086] They believed that was the end of Kush. They were wrong by nearly a thousand years.

## 6. The Kingdom That Kept Going

**THE GOD (V.O.)**

[B087] At Meroe, far up the Nile, where the summer rains came and iron ore lay near the surface, Kush built a different kind of world. There were furnaces, and heaps of iron slag that still stand today, some of the largest iron-working remains in all of ancient Africa. [B088] There were pyramids, small and steep and graceful, hundreds of them across the kingdom. [B089] There were temples to Apedemak, a lion-headed war god you will not find in Egypt. [B090] And Kush was ruled, again and again, by queens. The title was kandake. [B091] The Romans heard it and turned it into Candace, as if it were a single woman's name. It was not. It was a title, and the woman who made Rome respect it was named Amanirenas. [B092] In the year twenty-five before the common era, her armies swept north into Roman Egypt. The Romans struck back, and burned Napata. Amanirenas did not surrender. [B093] She sent envoys to the emperor Augustus, who was on the island of Samos, and he agreed to terms that left Kush independent and cancelled the tribute he had demanded. [B094] The Greek geographer Strabo sneered that she was mannish and blind in one eye. [B095] The emperor of Rome made peace with her anyway. [B096] Her warriors had also taken the bronze head of a statue of Augustus. [B097] They carried it south, and they buried it beneath the steps of a temple at Meroe. Many scholars believe it was placed there so that worshippers would tread over it. [B098] It lay there until nineteen ten, when archaeologists found it. [B099] The peace held for nearly three hundred years. Kush outlasted every native dynasty of Egypt. [B100] So why do you not know their names? That, at last, is the question worth asking.

## 7. The God's Reflection

**THE GOD (V.O.)**

[B101] Egypt had reasons to forget them. A kingdom that has been ruled by people it called foreigners does not tell that story kindly. [B102] In the surviving versions of the great list of pharaohs by the priest Manetho, Piye himself is missing. [B103] The masons cut away the rest. [B104] And the Greeks and Romans who came after knew Kush only as a distant land at the edge of the world. [B105] But Kush wrote itself, too. In a script that has never been fully deciphered. [B106] Meroitic, the language of Kush, still keeps its secrets. We can sound out the letters. We can recognize the names of kings and queens. But the sentences stay shut. [B107] I watched them speak it for more than a thousand years. I can tell you what they did. I cannot tell you what they said. [B108] History belongs to those who hold the chisel. But every chisel leaves a mark. [B109] Where Egypt cut away a king's name, it left a hole in the exact shape of the king. Scholars have been reading those holes for generations. [B110] The names are still in the stone. Someone only has to look. [B111] I have watched many empires try to forget their enemies. I have rarely watched one fail so completely.
