# VANISHED, EPISODE 1: EDWARD FURLONG

### Stardust Story | Full Documentary Script

**Estimated runtime:** 12 minutes

---

## [0:00] THE HOOK

At thirteen, Edward Furlong became one of the most recognizable young actors in the world.

He played John Connor in *Terminator 2: Judgment Day*, standing alongside Arnold Schwarzenegger in a film that would earn roughly half a billion dollars worldwide.

But twelve years later, when the franchise brought John Connor back, Furlong wasn't the one playing him.

Another actor got the role.

And the reason tells a much more complicated story than the usual tale of a child star who lost everything.

## [0:25] PREMISE STAMP

This is Stardust Story.

This is *Vanished*, episode one.

A series about the stars who walked away from the spotlight, or found themselves pushed out of it.

Fame creates stars.

Time turns them into dust.

## [0:45] THE BOY

Edward Furlong didn't arrive in Hollywood with years of acting experience behind him.

He was a teenager hanging around a Boys' Club in Pasadena when casting director Mali Finn noticed him.

Finn was searching for someone who could play John Connor, the boy at the center of James Cameron's *Terminator 2: Judgment Day*.

John needed to be streetwise, angry, funny, and believable. He couldn't feel like a child actor simply repeating lines.

Furlong had never appeared in a feature film.

He had to learn how to work on a major production, follow direction, hit his marks, and repeat scenes until they worked.

Then Cameron cast him.

He was thirteen.

Suddenly, a kid with no professional acting experience was working on one of the biggest productions of the decade.

There is a particular kind of luck in being discovered like that.

It opens doors most people never get to see.

But being given an extraordinary opportunity and being prepared for what comes next are two very different things.

Furlong was about to discover just how quickly a life could change.

## [1:50] THE EXPLOSION

*Terminator 2: Judgment Day* opened in the summer of 1991.

It became one of the year's biggest box-office hits, earning roughly half a billion dollars worldwide. It won four Academy Awards and helped redefine what audiences expected from a visual-effects blockbuster.

And at the center of it was a teenager who had never acted in a feature film before.

The audience loved John Connor.

Critics noticed Furlong's performance. He won an MTV Movie Award for his breakthrough role. He appeared in a Guns N' Roses music video. His face became instantly recognizable to millions of moviegoers.

Think about that transformation.

One year, you're an ordinary teenager.

The next, strangers recognize you in public. Adults want to know what you'll do next. Agents and producers start making promises about your future.

Suddenly, the world seems to be telling you that you've made it.

But there's a difference between becoming famous and knowing what to do with fame.

And when that happens at thirteen, you have very little time to figure it out.

## [2:55] THE NINETIES

Hollywood moved quickly.

There was *Pet Sematary Two*, followed by *American Heart*, where Furlong worked opposite Jeff Bridges. Then came *A Home of Our Own* and *Brainscan*.

These weren't all the same kind of role. Some were genre films. Others gave him opportunities to work with experienced actors and explore more complicated characters.

The important thing was that the boy from *Terminator 2* wasn't disappearing.

He was working.

He was learning.

He was growing up in an industry that had already formed an image of who he was supposed to be.

And that created a difficult problem.

Audiences had first met Furlong as a teenage John Connor. They remembered that face, that voice, that attitude.

But people grow up.

Their faces change. Their interests change. Their lives become more complicated.

For a former child star, growing up can feel like a performance that nobody has agreed to watch.

The audience wants the person it remembers.

The actor has to become someone new.

And the industry has to decide whether it still knows what to do with them.

## [4:05] THE MID-ROLL CALLBACK

We've seen versions of this story before.

Young stars rarely have one defining moment when everything falls apart.

More often, the changes happen gradually. Opportunities become less certain. Personal struggles become harder to manage. The world that once celebrated them starts paying attention for different reasons.

If you've watched our earlier stories, you'll recognize the pattern.

But Furlong's story takes an unexpected turn.

Because his strongest performance may have come after the world had already decided who he was.

## [4:40] THE COMEBACK THAT WAS REAL

In 1998, Furlong appeared in *American History X*, opposite Edward Norton.

He played Danny Vinyard, a young man growing up in a family shaped by racism, violence, and the consequences of hate.

It was a demanding film.

And Furlong demonstrated that he could handle serious adult material.

He wasn't simply repeating the role that had made him famous. He was playing a complicated character in a film that demanded much more than the rebellious attitude audiences remembered from *Terminator 2*.

He followed it with *Pecker*, directed by John Waters, and *Detroit Rock City*, which developed a following of its own.

For a moment, the story seemed to be moving in a different direction.

The child star was becoming an adult actor.

The career was evolving.

And the future still held possibilities.

Which makes what happened next more complicated than the familiar story of a young actor who simply failed to grow up.

## [5:50] THE FALL

Behind the scenes, Furlong was struggling.

He has spoken publicly about his history with drugs and alcohol, and about how his personal difficulties affected his life and career.

These struggles unfolded during years when he was trying to navigate adulthood, relationships, work, and the consequences of becoming famous so young.

We should be careful about pretending to know every reason a person develops an addiction.

We don't.

But we do know that Furlong's struggles became serious enough to affect his career.

By the early 2000s, his personal problems had become public knowledge. Legal difficulties and treatment entered the picture. Tabloid coverage increasingly focused on his struggles rather than his work.

And that creates a difficult cycle.

When an actor becomes associated with instability, producers may become more cautious about hiring them. Film productions operate on schedules and budgets. Delays can be expensive. Casting decisions often involve practical risks as well as artistic judgment.

For the person whose opportunities are disappearing, the result can be devastating.

The calls become less frequent.

The roles change.

The future that once seemed limitless begins to narrow.

And then Furlong lost the role that had made him famous.

## [7:15] THE RECASTING

*Terminator 3: Rise of the Machines* was coming together.

John Connor would return, this time as an adult.

But Edward Furlong wouldn't be playing him.

The role went to Nick Stahl.

Years later, Furlong spoke publicly about the drug-related incident that cost him the opportunity. He described the circumstances surrounding the signing of his contract and how his actions ultimately affected his chance to return.

It was a devastating career setback.

But the important point isn't that Furlong was replaced and therefore became a failure.

It's that a real person was dealing with serious problems while the franchise continued making business decisions.

The film went ahead.

The character continued.

And the actor who had introduced John Connor to a generation of moviegoers was left outside the story.

Imagine watching someone else step into the role that defined your childhood.

The character grows older.

The franchise moves forward.

Millions of people continue watching.

But you're no longer part of it.

For Furlong, losing John Connor was more than losing another acting job.

It was losing the role most closely associated with his identity as a performer.

## [8:15] THE LONG QUIET

After that, Furlong continued working.

There were independent films, smaller productions, and genre projects that attracted far less attention than his early work.

He hadn't disappeared completely.

But the scale of his career had changed.

And that's an important distinction.

When people talk about a star vanishing, they often imagine a dramatic exit. One scandal. One disastrous decision. One final headline.

Real life is rarely that simple.

Sometimes the work continues, but the audience stops paying attention.

The roles become smaller. The productions become less prominent. The public stops asking what comes next.

Eventually, people remember the old performances more clearly than they recognize the actor's current work.

The person is still there.

The image remains.

But the connection between them begins to fade.

For Furlong, John Connor remained the role that defined how much of the world remembered him.

And then, in 2019, that image returned to the big screen.

## [9:05] THE GHOST

*Terminator: Dark Fate* opened with a sequence that brought John Connor back.

Not as the adult actor Edward Furlong had become, but as a digital recreation of his younger appearance.

For a brief moment, the franchise returned to the image audiences remembered from 1991.

And then John Connor was killed.

The scene was shocking.

It also created an unsettling parallel with Furlong's career.

The character could be brought back exactly as audiences remembered him.

The actor himself had lived through nearly three decades of change, struggle, and experience.

The franchise could preserve the past on screen.

Real life could not.

For audiences, the scene was a dramatic return to the world of *Terminator 2*.

But it also raised an uncomfortable question.

What happens when a character becomes more valuable as a memory than as a living, changing person?

The digital recreation could bring back the boy.

It couldn't bring back the life he had lived since.

## [10:00] THE LENS

So what does the Edward Furlong story actually tell us?

It isn't simply a story about a young actor who made mistakes.

And it isn't proof that fame inevitably destroys everyone who experiences it.

That would be too easy.

Furlong had genuine talent. He delivered performances that went beyond the role that made him famous. He also experienced serious personal struggles that affected his life and career.

Both things can be true.

His story raises a larger question about the way we treat young performers.

We celebrate children when they become famous.

We reward them when they deliver performances beyond their years.

We turn them into recognizable faces, attach expectations to them, and build memories around the characters they play.

But the child eventually grows up.

The industry changes.

The audience moves on.

And the person underneath the public image still has to figure out how to live.

There is no simple formula for surviving that transition.

No guarantee that talent will protect someone from personal struggles.

No promise that early success will become lasting success.

And no reason to assume that a difficult chapter tells us everything about a person's life.

Furlong's story is not just about the role he lost.

It's about the person who continued after the role was gone.

## [10:55] TODAY

And yet, Edward Furlong is still here.

In 2025, he spoke publicly about reaching seven years of sobriety.

He has continued appearing at fan conventions, where people still line up to meet the actor who played John Connor.

They remember a boy who stood against an unstoppable machine and refused to give up.

But Furlong's life is more complicated than the character he played.

His career has taken turns nobody could have predicted in 1991. His personal struggles became part of a public story he never fully controlled.

And yet, his story is still being written.

Perhaps that is the part worth remembering.

Not the promise of a perfect career.

Not the fantasy that fame guarantees happiness.

But the possibility that a person can continue beyond the version of themselves the world remembers.

Sometimes vanishing isn't the end.

Sometimes it is simply the part of the story that takes longer to understand.
