ELDEN HENSON — They Cancelled His Character. Then They Brought Him Back. Then They Killed Him.
Playlist: The Defenders — Where Are They Now. TTS version as recorded; sections separated by "—— ——".


The show had been gone for seven years. When Netflix cancelled Daredevil in November 2018, it did not offer a finale, a sendoff, or a warning. The series simply stopped. And the characters inside it, the blind lawyer who fought crime in red body armor, his best friend who argued him back from the edge every time, the woman they both loved, all of them just went dark. Fans spent years wondering what would happen next. Then Disney Plus answered.

Daredevil: Born Again launched in March 2025 with the original cast intact. Charlie Cox was back as Matt Murdock. Deborah Ann Woll returned as Karen Page. And Elden Henson stepped back into the role that had defined the last decade of his career: Franklin Foggy Nelson, attorney at law, loyal partner, the moral compass of the whole show. The wait was over. The reunion was real.

Foggy Nelson did not survive episode one.

Before the first hour finished, Bullseye put a bullet in him. Henson watched the character he had spent three years building, across thirty-eight episodes, across four separate series, get erased in a single scene. He showed up at fan conventions afterward and joked about it. But the math was not hard to do. Elden Henson had been a working actor since 1982. He had been waiting, in one form or another, his entire career. And the moment the industry finally gave him something worth keeping, it took it away again.

This is how that pattern started. And this is why it keeps repeating.

Because some careers are not about arriving. They are about enduring.

—— ——

Elden Ryan Ratliff was born on August 30, 1977, in Rockville, Maryland. His father was a New York theatre actor. His mother was an educator and former photographer. The family moved to Burbank, California when he was still a toddler, and by the time he was two years old, he was working as a print model. By age six, he was appearing in commercials. By the time he started elementary school, he already had his SAG card.

Most people spend their childhoods becoming themselves. Elden Henson spent his becoming an actor. He appeared in a 1981 romantic comedy, then landed small roles in two films directed by Sidney Lumet, a director who had spent four decades at the center of American cinema. He was five years old. He appeared on the CBS daytime drama As the World Turns. He guest-starred on Amazing Stories in 1985 and Highway to Heaven in 1987. He played a child role in the 1988 ABC miniseries Elvis and Me, a role his older brother Erick played at age ten, meaning two brothers had inhabited the same character years apart. He had a role in Turner and Hooch in 1989.

None of these were the break. They were the apprenticeship.

He had been in the industry for a decade before most kids his age had their first job.

—— ——

In 1992, when he was fourteen years old, Elden Henson got the role that would follow him for the next thirty years. Disney was making a hockey movie about a ragtag team of underdogs coached by a reluctant lawyer played by Emilio Estevez. The Mighty Ducks needed an enforcer. A big kid with a shot so hard it was dangerous. A player nobody wanted to mess with, who turned out to have more heart than anyone expected.

Fulton Reed was that character. And Henson played him with exactly the kind of physical authority and understated warmth that made audiences fall for the film. The Mighty Ducks was a hit. A sequel, D2, followed in 1994. D3 came in 1996. Henson appeared in all three. His name was in the credits of a Disney franchise. He was a known face.

He briefly attended Emerson College in Boston to study acting formally. He lasted one semester before returning to California. The career could not wait.

He had been a franchise player before his eighteenth birthday. The question was what came after.

—— ——

What came after was fifteen years of solid work that no one quite remembers.

After the Mighty Ducks trilogy, Henson moved into the kind of roles that character actors accumulate like scar tissue: the best friend, the sidekick, the loyal supporting presence who makes the lead look better and rarely gets a scene of his own. He worked consistently. He was never unemployed for long. But the material had a ceiling.

In 1998 he appeared in The Mighty, a quietly affecting drama about an unlikely friendship between two boys. Critics noticed him. Then She's All That came in 1999, a Freddie Prinze Jr. vehicle that needed a best friend, and Henson filled the role. Idle Hands, same year. The Battle of Shaker Heights in 2003, alongside a young Shia LaBeouf. The Butterfly Effect in 2004 as Lenny, a character whose ordinariness was the point. Lords of Dogtown in 2005. Television guest spots on ER, Grey's Anatomy, Psych, Private Practice, Law and Order: Special Victims Unit. Always working. Always supporting.

Henson was aware of the pattern. He said in interviews that as an actor he was always pumped to get any job, that being a working actor required a lot of luck. He was not complaining. He was describing a reality. The industry had a clear category for him: the guy next to the guy.

For fifteen years, Elden Henson made other people's stories better. His own story was still waiting.

—— ——

In 2014, a second franchise found him.

The Hunger Games had become one of the defining young adult properties of the decade. The Mockingjay films, the two-part conclusion to the series, had a sprawling cast and needed a character named Pollux, a mute former Capitol cameraman who becomes part of the rebel cause. It was a supporting role. It was another franchise. It fit a pattern that had been established since 1992.

Henson appeared in Mockingjay Part One in 2014 and Part Two in 2015. His face was on the screen again in a film that hundreds of millions of people were watching. He was in his mid-thirties by this point, twenty-two years into a professional career, and the industry was still offering him the same slot it had offered him at fourteen: important to the story, not the center of it.

He took the job. He always took the job.

The difference between persistence and stubbornness is usually just timing.

—— ——

In 2015, Netflix launched Daredevil, and Elden Henson stepped into a role unlike anything his career had offered before.

Franklin Foggy Nelson was not a sidekick. He was not a best friend in the background. He was the conscience of the show, the person who forced Matt Murdock to account for himself, the voice that said out loud what the audience was thinking when the hero started making bad decisions. He was also funny, self-deprecating, loyal almost to a fault, and written with a specificity that gave Henson room to build something real.

He built it. Critics who came to the show expecting a Marvel action series came away talking about the Nelson and Murdock dynamic as the emotional engine of the whole thing. Henson played Foggy across all three seasons of Daredevil, across thirty-eight episodes. He reprised the role in The Defenders, in Luke Cage, and in Jessica Jones. He became, quietly, one of the most recognizable characters in the entire Marvel Netflix universe.

For three years, Elden Henson had a role worth keeping.

It was the best work of his career. And it ended without warning.

—— ——

On November 29, 2018, Netflix cancelled Daredevil. No finale was planned. No resolution was written. The show, along with every other Marvel Netflix series, was being cleared from the platform as Disney prepared to launch its own streaming service. The characters were frozen in contractual limbo. For years, nobody knew whether Matt Murdock or Foggy Nelson or any of them would ever appear again.

Henson had spent three years giving the best performance of his life. The industry thanked him with a press release.

He kept working. Sporadic television appearances. A guest role here, a guest role there. The years between 2018 and 2023 were quiet by the standards of what had come before. Then in 2023, Martin Scorsese cast him in Killers of the Flower Moon. The film was one of the most celebrated of the year, starring Leonardo DiCaprio and Robert De Niro, nominated for ten Academy Awards. Henson played Duke Burkhart, brother to DiCaprio's character. He appeared briefly. He had no lines. In a film three and a half hours long, his presence was measured in seconds.

The gap between the credit and the screen time said everything about where the industry still placed him.

—— ——

Then came the reversal. Disney Plus announced Daredevil: Born Again, a revival with Charlie Cox returning as Matt Murdock. Initially, the reports were grim. Henson and Deborah Ann Woll were not being brought back. The new version of the show was moving forward without the characters that had made the original matter. Fan outrage followed. The production went through a full overhaul, fired its original writing staff, and brought in new creatives to rebuild from scratch.

When the dust settled, Foggy Nelson was back. The announcement produced the kind of reaction online that reminded everyone how much the original show had meant. Set photos confirmed it. The law firm plaque on the wall of the new office read Nelson, Murdock, Page. The full reunion was happening.

Daredevil: Born Again premiered on Disney Plus in March 2025. The first episode was called Heaven's Half Hour. Within that hour, Wilson Bethel's Bullseye shot Foggy Nelson. Fans watched his funeral before the episode ended. Elden Henson, after seven years of waiting and a fight just to be included at all, had a single episode.

The showrunner acknowledged the weight of the decision. The creative team said it was not something they took lightly. The loss of Foggy was designed to reshape the entire universe of the show going forward. It was a narrative choice. It was also the latest in a long series of moments where the industry gave Elden Henson something important and then removed it.

Foggy Nelson had been cancelled twice. The second time, his own writers did it.

—— ——

Here is where the story refuses to end cleanly.

Following Foggy's death in Born Again, a complicated sequence of events unfolded at fan conventions. Henson told a crowd at LA Comic-Con in September 2025 that a return for season two was simply not happening. Fans processed the finality of it. Then Marvel TV boss Brad Winderbaum clarified at New York Comic-Con weeks later that Henson was in fact confirmed for season two, in some capacity. Henson had either not known, or had been playing coy with an audience that wanted honesty.

The character died. The actor came back. Exactly as Foggy Nelson himself has done repeatedly in the source material, exactly as Elden Henson has done throughout a career that should have stopped surprising people by now.

Season two of Born Again began filming, and Henson was in it. In April 2026, at a panel for the show, he was asked what he would like to see Foggy do going forward. He said he thought it would be cool if Foggy became an angel with wings and just flew around watching over Matt. He was joking. He was also, in the way that jokes sometimes are, completely serious.

The man has spent forty-four years in this industry. He has been a franchise child, a character actor, a cult favorite, a cancelled character, a reinstated character, a dead character, and a confirmed return. He has played in three Disney properties without once being the lead. He has outlasted the cancellation of a show he defined.

He is still here.

Some careers do not burn bright and go dark. Some careers just keep burning, and dare you to look away.
